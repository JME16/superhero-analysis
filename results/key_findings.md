# Key Analytical Findings

## Villain Threat Analysis

### Question 1: Which villains are associated with the greatest actual damage?

**Finding:** Darkseid ranks first with $4.7 billion in recorded
actual damage, followed by Brainiac at $2.4 billion, Doomsday
at $980 million, General Zod at $825 million, and Black Adam
at $410 million.

**Interpretation:** These villains represent the largest
cumulative damage exposure in the recorded missions.

**Limitation:** Total damage measures cumulative exposure,
not necessarily damage per incident. Mission frequency and
individual incident severity should be considered before
drawing conclusions about relative threat.


### Question 2: Which event types produce the largest financial exposure?

**Finding:** Planetary threats generated $7.1 billion in
recorded villain damage across two incidents, averaging
$3.55 billion per incident.

City-Scale Assault ranked second with $2.215 billion across
three incidents, averaging approximately $738.3 million
per incident.

**Interpretation:** Planetary threats lead because of
incident severity rather than frequency. Urban Combat
occurred five times but generated substantially less
damage. Geographic scope may help explain the difference,
but further evidence is required.

**Limitation:** Operating costs, repair costs, and villain
damage are reported separately. Combining these measures
without confirming their relationships could double-count
financial exposure.


## Mission Performance Analysis

### Question 3: Which mission types have the highest success rates?

**Finding:** Six of eleven mission types achieved a 100%
full-success rate:

- Investigation
- Reconnaissance
- Rescue
- Disaster Response
- Evacuation
- Patrol

Combat and Containment had the lowest full-success rates,
each at 33.3%, with one successful mission out of three.

**Discrepancy:** Although the calculations are correct,
mission types differ in difficulty and threat level.
Each category contains only two or three missions, so
the percentages should not be interpreted as definitive
measures of operational effectiveness.

Partial Success was not counted as full Success.


### Question 3A: Combat and Containment Investigation

**Finding:** Foxtrot was assigned two Very High threat
Combat missions and one High threat Containment mission.

The outcomes were:

- Combat: one Partial Success and one Failure
- Containment: one Partial Success

None of these three missions achieved full Success.

Bravo completed a Very High threat Combat mission
successfully, with $4.2 million in infrastructure damage.

Foxtrot's two Combat missions produced $6.9 million
and $12.5 million in infrastructure damage.

**Interpretation:** These results justify examining
Foxtrot's high-threat deployment effectiveness.

However, identical threat classifications do not
establish identical mission difficulty or conditions.


### Question 3B: Foxtrot Mission History

**Finding:** Foxtrot participated in four recorded missions:

- Mission 6: Containment, High, Partial Success
- Mission 13: Patrol, Low, Success
- Mission 20: Combat, Very High, Partial Success
- Mission 27: Combat, Very High, Failure

Foxtrot achieved full Success on its Low threat Patrol
mission but not on its three higher-threat assignments.

**Interpretation:** This pattern raises the possibility
of a mismatch between Foxtrot's capabilities and its
high-threat assignments.

The sample is too small to establish that Foxtrot is
incapable of handling such missions.


### Question 3C: Scheduling Investigation

**Hypothesis:** Foxtrot may have received high-threat
assignments because other teams were already deployed.

**Method:** A self-JOIN compared Foxtrot mission dates
against other teams' recorded mission dates.

**Finding:** No other teams had recorded missions on
the same dates as Foxtrot's four deployments.

**Interpretation:** The available records do not support
same-day mission overlap as an explanation for Foxtrot's
assignments.

**Limitation:** Absence of another recorded mission
does not prove that another team was available.
Readiness, personnel availability, and other operational
constraints were not established.


### Preliminary Operational Recommendation

Foxtrot's performance warrants management review,
particularly for Very High threat Combat assignments.

Three potential responses should be evaluated:

1. Targeted combat training followed by reassessment.
2. Restricting high-threat assignments and evaluating
   suitability for lower-threat or reconnaissance work.
3. Restructuring or disbanding Foxtrot if further
   evidence demonstrates persistent capability gaps
   that cannot reasonably be corrected.

**Current Recommendation:** Prioritize a combat readiness
review and targeted training. Consider temporarily
limiting Foxtrot's high-threat assignments until its
capabilities can be assessed.

Disbanding the team is not yet justified by four
recorded missions. Reconnaissance suitability also
remains unverified because Foxtrot's recorded success
was in Patrol, not Reconnaissance.


### Next Investigation

Compare Foxtrot's assigned personnel and expected
versus actual damage against Bravo's successful
Very High threat Combat mission.

Determine whether differences in personnel composition,
damage performance, or other recorded mission factors
provide additional evidence for a deployment decision.

The final recommendation should identify an actionable
response supported by the evidence, while acknowledging
the limits of the available data.

## Question 4: Expected vs. Actual Hero Damage

**Finding:** Across seven teams and 30 missions, Delta recorded the largest positive hero-attributed damage variance at +$1,613,681.50 (+20.1%). Foxtrot recorded +$64,010.93 (+2.7%).

**Delta Investigation:** Almost all of Delta's positive variance originated from Mission 11, an Extreme-threat Catastrophic Event that ended in Success. Delta successfully completed all four recorded missions. Its elevated variance therefore does not independently establish poor team performance.

**Foxtrot Investigation:** Foxtrot's overall variance concealed differences between individual missions. Mission 20 recorded -$13,786.21 and ended in Partial Success. Mission 27 recorded +$75,849.24 and ended in Failure.

**Personnel Analysis:** Foxtrot deployed four recorded heroes in Mission 20 and eight in Mission 27. Four heroes participated in both. Mission 27's individual positive and negative damage variances partially offset one another, demonstrating how aggregate results can conceal individual discrepancies.

**Operational Discrepancy:** Mission 27 involved higher recorded operating costs, infrastructure damage, repair costs, and personnel deployment than Mission 20, but achieved a worse outcome. The missions differed in location, adversary, personnel composition, and duration, limiting direct comparisons.

**Interpretation:** Hero-attributed damage variance alone does not explain mission success or failure. Mission difficulty, team composition, and other operational conditions must be considered when interpreting the results.

**Project Assumptions and Limitations:** Resource distribution was standardized by design in the dataset. This does not establish optimal allocation for individual missions. Detailed mission planning and deployment rationale are not available for analysis. These questions should be referred to a separate operational review rather than assigned speculative causes.

**Recommendation:** Prioritize a Foxtrot combat-readiness review and targeted training assessment before making permanent deployment or restructuring decisions. Further operational analysis is required to determine why Mission 27 failed.

**Analytical Principle:** An observed discrepancy justifies investigation but does not establish causation.
